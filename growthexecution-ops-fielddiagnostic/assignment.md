## The Case: A Manufacturer That Improved for 8 Years and Stayed Stuck

Study this before your field visit. It is a real engagement (identity withheld). It teaches you the pattern you will be hunting for.

A mid-size specialty manufacturer (roughly Rs.300Cr revenue) came to us with profit stuck well below target. Here is what we found in 3 days at their plant:

**Vignette 1 — The improvement paradox.** The plant had been reducing material rejection for 8 straight years. Real, measured improvement. Yet profit was flat. Why? Rejection is one of five waste types. It was the only one the plant measured, because it was caught at the quality gate. The other four were invisible: material lost during runs, runs that fell short and had to be repeated, overproduction sitting as inventory, and time lost to frequent machine changeovers. The plant was improving the one waste it could see. The other four could have been flat, growing, or enormous. Nobody knew.

**Vignette 2 — The real money lever wasn't efficiency.** The sales head told us 35% of customers were churning every year. Root cause: the plant could not tell a customer when an order would ship. Not price. Not quality. Delivery commitment. The plant's planning lived in two people's heads: a production head who batched orders by intuition built over years, and a planner doing three jobs who built each day's sequence from memory. Both were excellent. Neither could be predicted, scaled, or replaced. The biggest profit lever was making the plant predictable, not making it more efficient.

**Vignette 3 — Don't standardize what works.** The obvious consulting move is to impose rigid planning discipline. It would have been wrong. This plant won its market by accepting small, customized orders that competitors refused, which requires adaptive, flexible planning. The fix was to layer prediction and ownership on top of the flexible model, not replace it. Knowing what NOT to fix is half the diagnosis.

Carry these three questions into your field visit:
1. What does this business measure, and what losses does that measurement miss?
2. What can this business not predict or promise, and what does that cost them?
3. What looks like a flaw but is actually how they win?

# PART A: The Field Diagnostic

## Step 1 — Pick a real business you can physically visit

Requirements:
- It produces something or delivers a service through a repeatable process: a small manufacturer, printing press, tailoring unit, bakery, restaurant kitchen, auto workshop, packaging unit, clinic, laundry, furniture maker. A family business or a business where someone you know works are both ideal.
- You can observe the operation for at least 2 hours (one visit or several).
- You can get at least 30 minutes of conversation with the owner or the person who actually runs the floor.
- Prefer a business with a visible production or fulfillment step. Pure retail (buy-and-resell) makes the tasks harder for you.

If you genuinely cannot visit in person, a business where a family member works, diagnosed through detailed calls with them plus photos they send you, is an acceptable fallback. Say so explicitly and expect a higher bar on evidence.

State in one paragraph: what the business is, what it makes or does, roughly how big it is (people, daily output, or revenue if shared), and what your access was (who you talked to, how long you observed).

## Step 2 — Complete four diagnostic tasks

### Task 1: The Operation Map
Map the flow from "order comes in" to "customer receives it." Every step, who does it, where work waits. Mark the step where things pile up or slow down. Actual observed flow, with real names or roles, not a textbook diagram.

### Task 2: The Human System
In the case above, the plant's real planning system was two people's memory. Find the equivalent in your business. Who holds the schedule, the sequencing, the pricing, or the quality judgment in their head? What are they actually weighing when they decide (watch them decide, then ask)? What breaks on the day they are absent? Include at least two direct quotes from this person.

### Task 3: The Predictability Test
Ask the owner: "If I placed an order right now for [something specific and realistic], exactly when would it be ready?" Record the answer AND how the answer was produced (gut feel, a register, a phone call to someone, a system). Then find one recent promise the business missed: what was promised, what happened, what it cost them (a lost customer, a discount, a fight, a rushed night). If they claim they never miss, probe how they protect themselves (padding, refusing orders, keeping customers waiting to commit) and what THAT costs.

### Task 4: The Invisible Waste Hunt
Identify the one loss this business tracks (however informally). Then find at least three losses nobody measures. Candidates: material scrapped or reworked, time waiting for inputs or decisions, making more than needed, setup or switching time between jobs, work redone because the first attempt fell short, capacity idle while customers are refused. For each: what it is, your evidence (something you saw or counted, or a number the owner gave you), and your rough estimate of its size in the owner's units per day, week, or month. Estimates with visible logic beat missing numbers. Fabricated precision fails.

One document (md or PDF) in your repo containing:

# PART B: One Intervention

Design ONE intervention for the business you diagnosed. Pick a lever:

- **Predictability** — the business becomes able to commit and keep dates/times (an order register with realistic promise logic, a simple scheduling rule, a load board)
- **Ownership** — losses get caught where they happen, by the person doing the work, instead of being discovered at the end (an hourly self-check, a simple first-pass-right count per worker, a visible tally)

Your write-up (push to the same repo) must cover:

1. **The pick and the argument.** Why this lever over the other one, for THIS business, based on YOUR Part A evidence. One paragraph.
2. **The 4-week MVP.** What exactly gets recorded or done, by whom, taking how long per day (if it costs anyone more than 10 minutes a day, redesign it). Week-by-week.
3. **The signal.** At the end of week 4, what specific number or observable change tells the owner it is working? What result tells them it failed?
4. **The rejection risk.** The owner runs this business without you and has survived fine so far. What is the most likely reason they abandon your intervention by week 2, and what did you design to prevent that?

## Evaluation Criteria

1. **Observation quality** — Did you see the real operation, or write around it? Photos, quotes, and counted numbers are the tell.
2. **Waste-finding judgment** — Did you find losses the owner himself doesn't track? The best submissions surface something the owner found genuinely interesting when you named it.
3. **Evidence discipline** — Every claim traceable to something seen, heard, or counted. Estimates show their logic.
4. **Intervention realism** — Would this specific owner actually run your MVP for 4 weeks after you leave? Designs that need software, hiring, or the owner's personality to change score low.
5. **Respect for what works** — Did you spot the thing that looks broken but is actually why they win? (Vignette 3.)
6. **The variance rule** — Delete the business name. If the document still reads fine, it fails.
