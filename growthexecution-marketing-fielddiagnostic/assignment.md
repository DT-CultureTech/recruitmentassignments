## The Case: The Supplier Who Showed Up Too Late

Study this before your field visit. It is a real engagement (identity withheld). It teaches you the pattern you will be hunting for.

A mid-size specialty manufacturer (roughly Rs.300Cr revenue) was losing 35% of its customers every year despite good products and 20+ years in the market. Here is what we found:

**Vignette 1 — The lifecycle.** Every customer of this manufacturer goes through the same journey with THEIR own products: first they explore ideas ("what should we launch next?"), then they test feasibility with samples, then they run a small in-market pilot, then they scale to commercial volumes. Call these Stage 1 (ideation), Stage 2 (sampling), Stage 3 (pilot), Stage 4 (commercial). The manufacturer entered at Stage 4, every time. By then the customer's spec was fixed and 2-3 suppliers were being compared on price and delivery. At Stage 4 you compete. At Stage 1 you shape. The earlier you enter, the less competition exists and the higher the margin.

**Vignette 2 — The lost year.** A leading consumer brand sent this manufacturer a sample to match, with no context about what it was for. The manufacturer tried to match it blind (Stage 4 behavior), failed, and lost the business for over a year. If anyone had asked "what are you building, what does it need to do, what's your launch timeline?", their own R&D could have developed the right product. The capability existed. The entry point was wrong.

**Vignette 3 — Conversations are sensors.** When the sales team started entering at Stage 1 (sharing market intelligence, asking about the customer's product pipeline), a side effect appeared worth as much as the orders: customers revealed what they were planning 3-6 months before ordering. That forward signal let production plan capacity ahead of demand, which fixed the single biggest reason customers had been leaving: "they can't deliver when I need it."

Carry these three questions into your field visit:
1. At what stage of its customers' buying process does this business show up, and who got there earlier?
2. Which customers shrank or left, and what does the owner believe versus what is actually true?
3. What does this business know about its customers' future plans, and what does it only find out when the order (or the cancellation) arrives?

# PART A: The Field Diagnostic

## Step 1 — Pick a real business you can physically visit

Requirements:
- It has repeat customers whose individual identity matters: a small manufacturer or job-work unit, a printing press, a caterer, a wholesaler serving retailers, a tailor with regular clients, a coaching institute, a pharmacy supplying clinics, an event decorator. B2B or repeat-B2C both work. A one-time-walk-in business (highway dhaba, tourist shop) does not.
- You can get at least 45 minutes of conversation with the owner or whoever owns customer relationships.
- Bonus, not mandatory: you can also talk to one of their actual customers.

A family business or a business where someone you know works are both ideal. If you genuinely cannot visit in person, detailed calls with a family member who works there, plus supporting material they share, is an acceptable fallback. Say so explicitly and expect a higher bar on evidence.

State in one paragraph: what the business is, what it sells, roughly how big it is, who its customers are, and what your access was.

## Step 2 — Complete four diagnostic tasks

### Task 1: The Customer Map
Who are the top 5 customers or customer types by money? How does each order actually arrive (customer calls, owner visits, referral, standing arrangement)? Which single customer would hurt most to lose? Real names or clear role descriptions ("the school that orders uniforms every June"), with at least two direct quotes from the owner.

### Task 2: The Entry Point
Pick ONE real customer and reconstruct their buying journey for a recent purchase: when did their need first arise, what options did they consider, who did they talk to, and at what point did THIS business enter the picture? Map it to the four stages from the case. Then answer: who or what was already with that customer at an earlier stage (a competitor, a consultant, a supplier of something adjacent, Google)?

### Task 3: The Shrink-and-Leave Autopsy
Find one customer who stopped buying or visibly reduced their buying. What is the owner's explanation? Push past the first answer ("price" is almost never the real reason; the case above looked like a price problem and was a delivery-commitment problem). If you can reach that customer or someone who knows them, get their side. Record what the loss costs per year, in the owner's numbers or your estimate with visible logic.

### Task 4: The Wallet Share Probe
Take the business's best customer. Estimate: of everything that customer buys in this category, what share does this business get? What do they buy elsewhere, and from whom? Ask the owner directly, and note whether he actually knows. (Most owners have never asked. That itself is a finding.) What would it take to win 10-15% more of that customer's wallet?

## Part A Deliverable

One document (md or PDF) in your repo containing:
1. The business paragraph (Step 1)
2. The four tasks, each with its evidence: quotes, numbers, customer stories
3. At least 2 photos from the visit (the workplace, the order book, the counter; not the signboard)
4. A closing section, max half a page: "This business's biggest revenue leak is ___, and the owner does/doesn't see it because ___"

# PART B: One Earlier-Entry Play

Design ONE play that moves this business earlier in a real customer's buying process. Not a marketing plan. One play, for one named customer or one tightly defined customer type from your Part A.

Your write-up (push to the same repo) must cover:

1. **The pick and the argument.** Which customer, which stage the business enters at today, which earlier stage you are targeting, and why this customer over the others in your Part A. One paragraph.
2. **The value-first offer.** What does the business bring to that customer BEFORE the customer is ready to order? In the case above it was market intelligence the customer didn't have. For your business it might be something else: a trend the owner sees across all his customers, a cost-saving idea, a design suggestion, a heads-up on material prices. It must cost the business near nothing and be genuinely useful to the customer. "Discount" is an automatic fail: that is Stage 4 thinking.
3. **The actual conversation.** Write the opening 5-6 lines the owner would actually say to that customer, in the language they actually speak. A script for a real person, not a template. Rule from the case: the business's own products do not appear in the first half of the conversation.
4. **The signal to capture.** What forward information should the owner walk away with (the customer's next-3-months plans, an upcoming event, a growth intention), and where does he record it so it isn't lost?
5. **The 4-week test.** Owner runs this play with 3-5 customers over 4 weeks. What observable result says it is working? What says it failed? What is the most likely reason the owner stops doing it by week 2, and what did you design against that?

## Evaluation Criteria

1. **Listening quality** — Did you get past the owner's first answers? The best submissions contain a moment where the owner's story and the evidence disagree, and you noticed.
2. **Lifecycle judgment** — Did you correctly identify where the business enters, and is your earlier-entry target realistic for THIS business, or copied from the case?
3. **Evidence discipline** — Every claim traceable to a quote, a number, or a specific customer story. Estimates show their logic.
4. **Play realism** — Would this specific owner actually say those lines to that customer? Plays that require a CRM, a content team, or the owner becoming a different person score low.
5. **Signal thinking** — Does your play produce forward intelligence, or just a nicer sales pitch? (Vignette 3.)
6. **The variance rule** — Delete the business name. If the document still reads fine, it fails.
